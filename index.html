    Chunking for RAG: cut documents so the bot can answer

    A chatbot built on your documents can only answer from the pieces it finds. In this lesson you'll see how splitting documents into chunks decides what those pieces are, and you'll fix a bot that gets answers wrong because of bad chunking.

    About 45 minutes Beginner to intermediate No setup needed

    By the end, you will be able to

    1. Explain why RAG systems split documents into chunks, and what chunk size and overlap trade off.
    2. Compare five chunking strategies and pick one that suits a given document type.
    3. Clean and structure raw documents (PDF extracts, tables, headings) before chunking.
    4. Diagnose retrieval failures caused by chunking, and fix them.
    5. Measure chunking quality with an evaluation set and hit rate@k.

    Why did the bot get it wrong?

    2 min

    Meet the support bot for NimbusDesk, a fictional help-desk company. It answers customers using the company's own policy documents. A customer whose headset broke after five weeks asks a simple question:

    CustomerMy headset stopped working. Can I get a refund after 30 days?
    Support botI'm sorry, refunds are only approved within 30 days of delivery, so this order isn't eligible. ✗ Wrong. The policy has an exception for defective items.

    The model didn't "forget" the policy. It never saw the whole rule. Before answering, the bot ran retrieval: it searched the policy for the most relevant chunk and pasted that into the prompt. Here is what it got:

    What went wrong

    The document was cut into fixed-size pieces of 120 tokens. The cut landed in the middle of the exception clause, so the retrieved chunk stops at "…" and the condition (defective or damaged items) never reached the model.

    Key idea

    The LLM can only answer from what retrieval hands it, and chunking decides what retrieval can hand it. You'll fix this exact bug in section 6.

    02

    What chunking is, and why we need it

    6 min

    RAG in one picture

    Retrieval-Augmented Generation has two halves. Indexing happens once per document, ahead of time. Querying happens every time someone asks a question. Chunking sits in the middle of indexing, and everything after it inherits its decisions.

    Why chunk at all?

    Limited context window

    An LLM can only take in so many tokens per request (its context window). You can't paste a whole knowledge base into every prompt.

    Precise retrieval

    Search returns whole units. Small, focused units match a question precisely; a 40-page manual "matches" everything a little.

    Cost and speed

    Every token you send is paid for and processed. Sending three relevant paragraphs is cheaper and faster than sending thirty.

    Embedding input limits

    An embedding model also has a maximum input length. Text beyond it is usually truncated, so it never makes it into the vector.

    Analogy

    Chunking is like turning a textbook into index cards for revision. Make the cards too small and each one loses its meaning ("It costs ₹499." What does?). Make them too big and you can't find the one fact you need quickly.

    Tokens, not characters

    Models don't count characters or words. They count tokens, the pieces a tokenizer cuts text into. A common English word is often one token; rarer words, numbers and symbols split into several. A rough rule of thumb for English is about 4 characters per token, but it varies by tokenizer and language. Type below to see it live.

      The core trade-off: chunk size

      Chunk size is a balance, and both extremes fail in different ways:

      • Too small: fragments lose their context ("It costs ₹499 per month." Which plan?), and one answer gets scattered across several chunks.
      • Too large: one chunk covers many topics, so its embedding is a blurry average and matches are less precise. You also send more irrelevant text to the LLM.

      Try it: find the sweet spot

      Question: "How much is the Team plan?"

      Fixed-size chunks with no overlap, measured in tokens. Drag the slider and watch which chunk holds the price.

      Overlap: a safety margin at the edges

      Chunk overlap repeats a little text from the end of one chunk at the start of the next. If an important sentence sits on a boundary, it will usually appear whole in at least one of the two chunks.

      no overlap
      1 2 3
      overlap
      1 2 3 4

      Hatched areas are text that appears in two chunks.

      Overlap isn't free: you get more chunks, more storage, and some duplicate text in your results. Each new chunk only moves forward by chunk_size − overlap, so the overlap must be smaller than the chunk size. Otherwise the splitter would never move forward. A modest overlap, often somewhere around 10–20% of the chunk size, is a common starting point to tune from.

      Key idea

      Chunks are the units your system can find and the LLM can read. Size trades context against precision; overlap protects the boundaries at the cost of duplication. Count both in tokens.

      03

      Chunking strategies explained

      5 min

      All strategies answer the same question: where is it OK to cut? Each mini-preview below runs the real chunker from this lesson on the same short pricing snippet. Colours and numbers mark the chunks.

      chunk colours (number badges mark each start) · overlap

      1Fixed-size

      Cut every N tokens (or characters), optionally with overlap. Simple and predictable, and it ignores meaning completely, so it happily cuts mid-sentence.

      Best forQuick baselines, uniform text, logs
      Watch outBroken sentences, split tables

      2Sentence-based

      Split into sentences, then pack whole sentences into each chunk until the size limit. Boundaries always fall between sentences.

      Best forProse, articles, transcripts
      Watch outVery long sentences; ignores headings

      3Recursive

      Try separators in order: paragraph \n\n → line \n → sentence . → word " " → character. Only go finer when a piece is still too big, then merge small pieces up to the limit. A popular general-purpose default.

      Best forMost text, as a first choice
      Watch outDoesn't know tables or headings mean anything

      4Structure-aware

      Follow the document's own structure: split at Markdown/HTML headings, keep tables and code blocks whole, and save the heading path (e.g. Pricing › Plans) as metadata.

      Best forDocs sites, manuals, READMEs, pricing pages
      Watch outNeeds clean structure; huge sections still need sub-splitting

      5Semantic advanced

      Embed every sentence, compare neighbours with cosine similarity, and start a new chunk where the meaning shifts (the similarity drops below a threshold).

      1Starter is free for one agent. It keeps 30 days of history. 2You can pay by card or UPI. Failed payments are retried after 3 days.

      Illustration: the topic changes from plans to payments, so a break goes there. Try the real thing in the Chunk Lab (it downloads a small model).

      Best forLong, flowing text that changes topic
      Watch outSlower, needs an embedding model, threshold to tune

      Three more ideas worth knowing

      • Parent–child (small-to-big) retrieval: search over small chunks for precision, then hand the LLM the larger parent section the match came from, for context.
      • Contextual chunk headers: prepend the document title and section path to each chunk (or only to the text you embed/index) so a lone chunk still makes sense.
      • Domain-specific splitting: code by function or class, chat logs by conversation turns, FAQs by question-and-answer pair.
      Key idea

      Better strategies cut where the document's meaning allows it: between sentences, at headings, around tables, at topic shifts. Start with recursive or structure-aware, and add context (headers, parents) when chunks lose meaning on their own.

      04

      Document processing before chunking

      5 min

      Chunking can only be as good as the text it receives. In real projects, most "bad chunk" problems start one step earlier.

      1. Parsing: get the text out

      Parsing (or extraction) pulls text from PDFs, web pages, Word files and slides. Scanned PDFs are just images, so they need OCR first. Layout matters: a two-column page read straight across mixes sentences from both columns, and tables often come out as a jumble of cells.

      2. Cleaning: remove the noise

      • Remove repeated headers, footers and page numbers (boilerplate).
      • Fix words hyphenated across line breaks ("infor-↵mation" → "information").
      • Normalise whitespace: collapse extra spaces and unwrap hard line breaks inside paragraphs.
      • Strip web clutter such as navigation menus and cookie banners.

      3. Preserve structure
      
        
      Headings, lists, tables and code blocks carry meaning, so don't flatten them into one blob. Tables are the classic failure: once rows are flattened and split, a value loses its column header.
      
        
      
          
      
            
      Kept as a table
      
            
      
              
      Add-onPrice per monthAvailable on
      
              
      Extra storage₹300All plans
      Phone channel₹2,500Business
      
            
      
          
      
          
      
            
      Flattened and split
      
            
      …Extra storage ₹300 All plans Phone channel
      
            
      ₹2,500 Business SSO and audit log ₹1,800…
      
            
      The second chunk has no idea "₹2,500" is a monthly price, or which add-on it belongs to.
      
          
      
        
      

        
      4. Attach metadata to every chunk
      
        
      Store useful fields with each chunk. Metadata lets you filter ("only HR policies"), cite sources ("Refund policy, section 1"), and check freshness ("updated 2026-03-04").
      
        
      {
  "text": "Refunds after 30 days are allowed only if the item is defective…",
  "source": "refund-policy.md",
  "page": 1,
  "section_path": ["Refund & Returns Policy", "1. Standard refunds"],
  "doc_type": "policy",
  "last_updated": "2026-03-04"
}
      

        
      Key idea
          
      Garbage in, garbage chunks out. Parse carefully, clean away the noise, keep structure, and attach metadata before you choose a chunk size.

      Code you can use
      
        
      In Python, the langchain-text-splitters package implements several of these strategies. The Chunk Lab's Export as code button writes a snippet that matches your current settings. Here is a typical starting point:
      
        
      # pip install langchain-text-splitters tiktoken
from langchain_text_splitters import RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter

splitter = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter.from_tiktoken_encoder(
    encoding_name="cl100k_base",  # measure chunk_size in tokens
    chunk_size=300,
    chunk_overlap=45,             # 15% overlap
)
chunks = splitter.split_text(document_text)
      
        
      For Markdown, split on headings first, then size-limit each section:
      
        
      from langchain_text_splitters import MarkdownHeaderTextSplitter, RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter

header_splitter = MarkdownHeaderTextSplitter(
    headers_to_split_on=[("#", "h1"), ("##", "h2"), ("###", "h3")],
    strip_headers=False,  # keep the heading text inside each chunk
)
sections = header_splitter.split_text(markdown_text)  # Documents with heading metadata

size_splitter = RecursiveCharacterTextSplitter.from_tiktoken_encoder(
    encoding_name="cl100k_base", chunk_size=400, chunk_overlap=40
)
chunks = size_splitter.split_documents(sections)  # metadata is carried over
      

        
      Real-world scenarios
      
        
      
          
      Customer-support bot
      Help articles and policies full of exceptions ("unless", "only if"). Boundaries and overlap matter most: never separate a rule from its exception.
      
          
      Internal knowledge base
      HR and IT policies in many formats. Cleaning PDFs and storing metadata (department, last updated) lets you filter and keep answers current.
      
          
      Legal and contract Q&A
      Split by clause, keep clause numbers in metadata, and cite them. A clause cut in half can reverse its meaning.
      
          
      Developer docs and code assistant
      Structure-aware splitting for docs; function- or class-level splitting for code, with the file path as metadata. Never split a code block.
      
          
      Healthcare or finance documents
      Answers need citations and often filters (document type, date, region). Rich metadata and conservative chunk sizes help auditability.
      
        
      

        
      Common mistakes
          
        
            
      • Chunks so small that they lose their meaning.
        • 
            
      • No overlap on prose, so answers get cut at boundaries.
        • 
            
      • Ignoring tables and headings (flattening structure).
        • 
            
      • Not storing metadata, so no citations or filters.
        • 
            
      • One configuration for every document type.
        • 
            
      • Never evaluating retrieval, just trusting that it "looks fine".
        • 
            
      • Mixing up token and character counts (a "500" limit means very different things).
        • 
          
      
        
      

        
      Key idea
          
      Match the strategy to the document type, start from sensible defaults, then let an evaluation set (not intuition) choose the final settings.
